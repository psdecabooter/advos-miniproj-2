\section{Introduction}

The UNIX pipe is the oldest IPC mechanism still in daily use, and its cost
model is usually taken on faith: sixteen pages of buffer, writers block when
it fills, larger writes amortize better. We measured those claims and then
asked where the time goes.

The dominant variable turns out to be \emph{placement}, not payload size. Two
processes exchanging four bytes cost \SI{708}{ns} per message on one core and
\SI{2042}{ns} across two, for identical work and identical system-call counts;
the difference is entirely time spent descheduled. The same asymmetry governs
bandwidth: the familiar throughput collapse at the pipe capacity happens only
across cores. We establish capacity as its cause by varying it directly.

\section{Apparatus and method}

An Ubuntu 24.04 guest on a custom Linux 7.2.4 kernel (arm64), eight vCPUs,
\SI{12}{GiB} RAM, under QEMU/\texttt{hvf} on an Apple M1. Page size is
\SI{4}{KiB} and \texttt{pipe-max-size} is \SI{1}{MiB}, so the resize sweep
below runs unprivileged. Benchmarks are C at \texttt{-O3 -Wall -Wextra}.
Absolute figures include hypervisor overhead; the comparisons between
placements do not depend on it.

Processes are placed with \texttt{sched\_setaffinity} after the
\texttt{fork}, not with \texttt{taskset}, which imposes a single shared mask
and cannot express ``parent on core 0, child on core 1''. Each configuration
is launched five times; each launch reports its internal minimum and we take
the minimum again across launches, since noise can only add time. Throughput
is a rate, so we report the maximum and never an arithmetic mean.

\section{Choosing a clock}

\textbf{Variables.} Cause: the clock API. Effects: accuracy, call cost,
granularity. \textbf{Hypothesis.} \texttt{clock\_gettime} can resolve a single
exchange; \texttt{gettimeofday} cannot.

We compared \texttt{clock\_gettime(CLOCK\_MONOTONIC)}, \texttt{gettimeofday},
and \texttt{cntvct\_el0}, this architecture's analogue of \texttt{rdtsc}.
Timing \texttt{sleep(5)} five times, all three recovered \SI{5.0024}{s} and
agreed within \SI{53}{ppm}; the \SI{492}{ppm} excess is \texttt{sleep}'s own
wakeup latency, which is precisely why the clocks agree on it.

Granularity came from $10^6$ back-to-back reads into a preallocated array,
post-processed afterwards, with nothing else in the loop
(Table~\ref{tab:clocks}). The decisive column is the last:
\SI{97.45}{\percent} of adjacent \texttt{gettimeofday} readings are
\emph{identical}, two calls finishing inside one microsecond tick. A clock
that cannot separate events by its own call cost cannot measure a
\SI{708}{ns} round trip, so we discarded it.

The cycle counter is cheapest to read but not finest: \texttt{cntfrq\_el0}
reports \SI{24}{MHz}, a \SI{41.67}{ns} period.
\texttt{clock\_gettime} shows that same \SI{41}{ns} quantum because
\texttt{current\_clocksource} is \texttt{arch\_sys\_counter} --- the vDSO
reads that very register and scales it. It costs three times more per call and
gives up no resolution, so we used it throughout. Neither standard
cycle-counter caveat bites here: frequency invariance follows from the
\texttt{sleep} agreement, and reading \texttt{cntvct\_el0} on each of eight
cores showed only \SI{400}{ns} of noise with no systematic step, the arm64
generic timer being system-wide rather than per-core.

\section{Latency}

\textbf{Variables.} Cause: payload size and placement. Effect: one-way
latency, taken as half a round trip so only one clock on one core is involved.
\textbf{Hypothesis.} Flat while fixed cost dominates, then linear once copying
does.

Figure~\ref{fig:latency} confirms that shape and adds one the hypothesis
missed. Below \SI{4}{KiB} every curve is flat: cost is per exchange, not per
byte. Above \SI{16}{KiB} growth is linear, and the same-core slope implies
\SI{19.2}{GiB/s} of copy bandwidth --- a round trip copies the payload twice
while the plotted value is half a round trip, so the factors of two cancel.

The surprise is the constant offset in the flat region: \SI{708}{ns}
same-core against \SI{2042}{ns} cross-core, a payload-independent penalty of
\SI{1333}{ns}. Unpinned runs track the cross-core curve, so the scheduler
spreads the pair by default. Section~\ref{sec:time} accounts for that penalty.

\section{Throughput}

\textbf{Variables.} Cause: chunk size and placement. Effect: sustained one-way
bandwidth, acknowledged once for the whole transfer. \textbf{Hypothesis.}
Throughput rises as the fixed per-write cost amortizes, then falls once chunks
exceed capacity and the writer blocks.

Figure~\ref{fig:thr}(a) shows the first half right and the second wrong. Only
the cross-core curve collapses, peaking at \SI{6431}{MiB/s} at \SI{8}{KiB} and
falling to \SI{2391}{MiB/s} by \SI{512}{KiB}. The same-core curve climbs to
\SI{9678}{MiB/s} and stays flat through chunks eight times the capacity. That
falsifies blocking as the explanation: the same-core writer blocks just as
often, the pipe being the same size, and pays nothing for it.

The mechanism is loss of \emph{overlap}. Across cores, writer and reader truly
run in parallel, and that pipelining survives only while the writer can
deposit a whole chunk without stalling. Past capacity it stalls mid-chunk
every time, the pair falls into lockstep, and every handoff costs a full
cross-core wakeup. On one core there was no parallelism to lose, so bandwidth
simply saturates at what the cache hierarchy sustains. The crossover at
\SI{4}{KiB} follows: below it overlap beats locality, above it locality wins.

\section{Where the time goes}
\label{sec:time}

Filling a nonblocking pipe until \texttt{EAGAIN} accepts exactly
\SI{65536}{bytes}, matching both \texttt{F\_GETPIPE\_SZ} and the sixteen-page
folklore; single-byte writes reach the same total, so no page is wasted per
write.

Table~\ref{tab:time} accounts for the wall clock with \texttt{getrusage}. At
\SI{4}{B} the same-core pair spends essentially all its time in the kernel and
none blocked. The cross-core pair does identical work --- \texttt{strace}
confirms exactly two \texttt{write} and two \texttt{read} calls per round trip
--- yet is ten times slower, with \SI{61}{\percent} of its wall clock in
neither user nor system time. That residue answers the \SI{1333}{ns}
question: it is time descheduled, awaiting a wakeup on another core.

The context-switch column closes the loop with capacity: $1.99$ voluntary
switches per round trip at \SI{4}{B} and $15.99$ at \SI{512}{KiB}, exactly
$2\lceil \text{size}/\text{capacity}\rceil$ against the measured
\SI{64}{KiB}. \texttt{strace} gives the same arithmetic from the other side
--- at \SI{512}{KiB} the reader issues nine \texttt{read} calls per round
trip, eight for data and one for the ack, because a read returns only what is
buffered. Capacity sets the number of handoffs; placement sets the price of
each.

Finally we made capacity the independent variable.
Figure~\ref{fig:thr}(b) re-runs the cross-core sweep with
\texttt{F\_SETPIPE\_SZ} from \SI{4}{KiB} to \SI{1}{MiB}. The knee follows it:
a \SI{16}{KiB} pipe collapses at \SI{8}{KiB} chunks, a \SI{64}{KiB} pipe at
\SI{64}{KiB}, a \SI{256}{KiB} pipe at \SI{256}{KiB}, and a \SI{1}{MiB} pipe,
larger than any chunk tested, never collapses. The correlation is causal.

\section{Conclusions}

Pipe cost is set by two quantities, and payload size is only one of them.
Capacity fixes how many handoffs a transfer needs, exactly
$2\lceil \text{size}/\text{capacity}\rceil$ voluntary context switches,
confirmed against a measured \SI{64}{KiB} and then verified causally by moving
it. Placement fixes the price of each handoff: \SI{1333}{ns} of descheduled
time when the peer is on another core, three quarters of the total at small
payloads.

The practical consequence is counterintuitive. Spreading a communicating pair
across cores buys parallelism worth having only while messages stay below
capacity; past that, the pair is four times faster confined to a single core.
A benchmark that leaves placement to the scheduler measures the cross-core
case without saying so.

Two limits bound these results: everything ran virtualized, so absolute
latencies carry hypervisor overhead, and no PMU was available in the guest, so
Table~\ref{tab:time} rests on \texttt{getrusage} and \texttt{strace} rather
than hardware counters. We report the residue as blocked because it is neither
user nor system time, not because a counter placed it there.
